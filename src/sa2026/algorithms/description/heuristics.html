Heuristics: the guess h(n)

Intelligent Search Algorithms · Latakia University · Lesson 1 of 3: Heuristics → Greedy → A*

What is a heuristic?

You are in a new city. You can see a tall tower far away, and you want to walk to it. You do not know the streets, but you can say: "the tower is about 2 km from here".

This is a guess. It is not exact, but it helps you choose the next street. BFS, DFS and UCS are uninformed (blind): they do not know where the goal is. The next algorithms (Greedy and A*) are informed: they use a guess to choose the next node.

A heuristic h(n) = a guess of the cost from node n to the goal.
Rules: h(n) ≥ 0, and at the goal h(goal) = 0.

Three letters to remember

g(n)The real cost from the start to n. We already paid it, so we know it.
h(n)A guess of the cost from n to the goal.
h*(n) ("h star")The real cheapest cost from n to the goal. We do not know it during the search. (If we knew it, we would not need to search!)

Think: a GPS app gives these guesses. Look at B: the guess is 4 km, but the real cost is 9 km. Is that a problem? Look at C: the guess is exactly right.

A good heuristic is: (1) close to the real cost h*, and (2) quick to compute.

Is a guess "good enough"? There are two tests: admissible (step 2) and consistent (step 3).

Admissible = never too big

A heuristic is admissible if it never overestimates: the guess is never bigger than the real cost.

h(n) ≤ h*(n) for every node n

An admissible heuristic is optimistic. It may say "you are close" when you are far, but it never says "you are far" when you are close.

Why do we care? A* trusts the guess. If the guess is too big on the best path, A* thinks the best path is bad and may never take it.

Consistent = no big jumps

A heuristic is consistent if, on every road, the guess goes down by at most the cost of that road.

h(n) ≤ c(n, n') + h(n')

for every node n and every child n'. And h(goal) = 0.

It is the triangle rule: the direct side h(n) can not be longer than the way around: c(n, n') + h(n').

Real life: at home, the GPS says "10 km to the mall". You drive 1 km to your friend's house, and now the GPS says "3 km". Strange! You drove 1 km, but you came 7 km closer? 10 > 1 + 3, so the guesses are not consistent.

How to check one road

Drive from A to C. You drove 5 km. The guess went from 8 down to 3: it dropped by 5. You drove 5 km, so you can be at most 5 km closer. 5 ≤ 5 ✓ OK

Drive back from C to A. The guess goes up (3 → 8). Going up is always OK, so there is nothing to check.

The rule for every road: the drop of the guess can not be bigger than the length of the road.
drop = bigger h − smaller h   must be   ≤ road length

Not OK: the guess "drops too fast" says h(C) = 0. From A to C the drop is 8 − 0 = 8, but the road is only 5 km. You can not come 8 km closer by driving 5 km. ✗ not consistent

This is the formula from the top: h(A) ≤ c(A, C) + h(C) → 8 ≤ 5 + 3.

Consistent ⇒ admissible

Walk on the cheapest path from n to G. On each road the guess drops by at most the road cost. It drops from h(n) down to h(G) = 0, so h(n) ≤ the sum of the road costs = h*(n).

So: every consistent heuristic is also admissible. But not the other way! Click "small map" on the right: admissible, but not consistent.

Small tasks

  1. Start from the GPS guess. What is the biggest h(B) that is still admissible? Is it still consistent?
    h(B) = 9, because h*(B) = 9. But the road B – E breaks consistency: 9 − 3 = 6 > 4. So it is admissible, but not consistent.
  2. Click "h = 0". Is it admissible? Consistent? Is it useful?
    Yes and yes. Every city has h = 0, so on every road the drop is 0 − 0 = 0.
    Careful: this is not like "drops too fast". There, only h(C) is 0 and its neighbour A still has h = 8, so the drop on the road A – C is 8 > 5. The value 0 is not the problem. A big jump between two neighbours is the problem.
    Is h = 0 useful? No: every city looks the same, so it gives no information. Later you will see: with h = 0, A* is just UCS.
  3. Try to make a guess that is consistent but not admissible (keep h(G) = 0).
    You can not! Consistent ⇒ admissible (step 3).
  4. What happens if h(G) is not 0?
    It is not admissible and not consistent. At the goal the real cost is h*(G) = 0, so any h(G) > 0 is too big: not admissible. Consistent needs h(G) = 0, and consistent ⇒ admissible, so it can not be consistent either. Try it in the lab: put h(G) = 2.

The big picture

Consistent is the stronger test: it is inside admissible. Most guesses we use (straight line, Manhattan) are consistent.
